How fancy text works, and where it breaks

It is not a font. Apps like Instagram, TikTok, X and Discord only let you type plain characters, so a font cannot come with your text. What does come with it are different characters that happen to look like styled letters. Unicode 3.1 (2001) added a whole block of “Mathematical Alphanumeric Symbols” — bold, italic, script, Fraktur, double-struck, sans-serif and monospace alphabets — so mathematicians could write 𝐱 and 𝑥 as different symbols. This tool swaps each letter for its look-alike from that block and from a few others: circled and squared letters, small capitals borrowed from phonetic alphabets, superscripts, and “combining” marks that draw a line through, under or over the letter before them.

Why a few letters look slightly different. Some styled letters existed before 2001 in the older “Letterlike Symbols” block — ℎ (the Planck constant), ℬ, ℭ, ℂ, ℍ, ℝ and others — so Unicode left holes in the new alphabets and points to the old ones. The tool uses the official characters for those holes; generators that skip this step produce empty boxes.

Where it breaks. A phone or computer without a glyph for a symbol shows a box instead, most often for upside-down letters and the rarer superscripts. There is no superscript Q or F in Unicode at all, and only seventeen subscript letters, so those styles leave the missing letters plain. Screen readers may announce 𝐇 as “mathematical bold capital H” or ignore it, and search engines and in-app search treat 𝐡𝐞𝐥𝐥𝐨 as a different word from hello — lovely for decoration, poor for anything someone needs to read or find.

Character limits. Each fancy letter is one symbol to you, but most of these live outside the basic range and are stored as two units, and some apps count them that way. A bio or name with a limit fills up faster in fancy text than in plain.

Privacy. Everything is converted in your browser as you type. Your text is not saved or sent; only your favourite styles and chosen frame are remembered on this device.
