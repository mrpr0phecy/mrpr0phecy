How to get accurate readings, calibration, and what the units mean

Two ways to hold the phone. Lay it flat on a shelf or worktop and the round bubble shows both directions at once, like a bullseye level; the bubble drifts to the high side, exactly as a real one does. Stand the phone on its long edge against a picture frame, a fence post or a door and the display switches to a single vial reading the tilt of that edge. Take the case off if it has a bumpy back, and remember the phone measures its own body, so it is only as flat as the surface it rests on.

Calibration. Phone sensors have a small fixed offset — often half a degree — so a fresh phone can report a truly level table as slightly off. Put the phone on a surface you trust, tap Calibrate here, and that position becomes zero; the correction is remembered on this device until you clear it. To check any surface without a reference, use the turn-around test: take a reading, rotate the phone 180° on the same spot, and read again — the true tilt is the average of the two, and half the difference is the sensor’s offset.

Units. Degrees are the sensor’s own language. Percent grade is rise over run × 100, the figure on road signs and in accessibility rules (a 1 in 12 ramp is 8.3%). mm per metre is what plumbers and landscapers use for drainage falls — a soil pipe needs roughly 18–25 mm/m, a patio about 15 mm/m away from the house. Roof pitch gives inches of rise per 12 inches of run, as used on drawings and by shingle manufacturers; a 30° roof is close to 7 in 12. Fall ratio expresses the same slope as 1 in x.

Precision. Phone accelerometers resolve about 0.1°, but readings jitter, so the display is smoothed and Hold freezes it so you can write it down. The bubble turns green and the phone buzzes (if it can) when both directions are within half a degree of level. On iPhones the browser asks permission to use motion sensors the first time; on a desktop computer without sensors you can drag the bubble to see how the display behaves.
