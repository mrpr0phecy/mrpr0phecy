The elements in it

    How it works, why some words are impossible, and the letters to avoid

    What it does. Every element has a one- or two-letter symbol, and the tool tries every way of chopping your word into those symbols: bacon is Ba–Co–N (barium, cobalt, nitrogen), genius is Ge–Ni–U–S, and a name like Erica is Er–I–Ca. When more than one split works you get them all: snack is both Sn–Ac–K and S–Na–C–K. When none works — Chris fails because after C and H there is no element R or Ri — the tool shows the best it can do, with the letters no element covers shown as plain grey dashed tiles.

    Why some words fail. Two letters of the alphabet appear in no element symbol at all: J and Q. Any word containing them is impossible. The single-letter elements are H, B, C, N, O, F, P, S, K, V, Y, I, W and U; every other letter has to be part of a two-letter symbol, so a word succeeds or fails on its letter pairs. E is the usual killer — no element is just E, and only Be, Ce, Fe, Ge, He, Ne, Re, Se, Te, Xe, Er, Es and Eu contain it — which is why coffee and science cannot be done while Heisenberg (He–I–Se–N–Be–Rg) can. W, X and Z only ever start a symbol (W, Xe, Zn, Zr), so a word ending in X or Z is impossible, while one ending in W is fine thanks to tungsten.

    Numbers and families. Each tile carries the element's atomic number (how many protons it has), its symbol and its full name; the family colours follow the usual periodic-table groups — alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, transition metals, post-transition metals, metalloids, reactive non-metals, halogens, noble gases, lanthanides and actinides. The list under the tiles gives the same detail in words, so the picture doubles as a small chemistry lesson.

    Sharing. The PNG is drawn at twice the on-screen size, so it prints or posts cleanly; the link just carries the word, so whoever opens it sees the same tiles. Everything runs in your browser and nothing is stored except the tile colour you picked.
