Motion blur & ghosting

Follow each pattern with your eyes. Sharp checks mean clear motion; a smear shows sample-and-hold blur; a faint trailing copy or a bright/dark halo is ghosting or overshoot.

Black & white levels

On a well-set screen you can just make out the first few squares in both strips. If roughly the first sixteen black squares and the last near-white ones all vanish, the computer and screen probably disagree about RGB range (full vs limited) — common over HDMI. If blacks look grey and washed out, it is the opposite mismatch.

Gradient banding

Each ramp is drawn in 256 equal steps. On a good 8-bit screen they look almost smooth; clear, evenly spaced stripes suggest a 6-bit panel, a low colour-depth setting or heavy picture processing.

How each test works, and its limits

Refresh rate. Browsers draw animation frames in step with the screen, so the tool times about two seconds of frames, takes the typical gap between them and converts it to hertz, then names the nearest common mode (60, 120, 144, 165, 240 Hz…) when it is within 3%. It measures the screen this window is on, at its current setting — if a 144 Hz monitor reads 60, check the operating system's display settings and the cable. Safari on iPhone and iPad caps web animation at 60 fps by default, so ProMotion screens read 60 there, and battery-saver modes can halve the rate. Variable-refresh (FreeSync, G-Sync) screens report the rate the browser is driving at.

Dead and stuck pixels. A dead pixel stays dark on every colour and is easiest to spot on white; a stuck pixel shows one bright colour and is easiest to spot on black. Clean the screen first — dust looks exactly like a dead pixel until you wipe it. Check your retailer's or maker's pixel policy before you return a screen; many only replace a panel above a set number of faulty pixels. This tool has no flashing “pixel fixer”: rapid full-screen flashing can trigger seizures, and the evidence that it revives pixels is thin.

Backlight bleed and uniformity. Turn the room lights off and the brightness up. Light leaking in from the edges or corners that stays put when you move your head is bleed; a glow that shifts with your viewing angle is normal “IPS glow”. The grey screens show patchy brightness and tints.

Motion blur. Most screens hold each frame until the next one (“sample and hold”), so a moving object smears across roughly the distance it travels in one frame: at 60 Hz a 960 px/s object smears about 16 pixels, at 144 Hz about 7. That is why higher refresh rates look clearer. Lanes move at 1×, 2× and 4× the base speed. A faint trailing copy is ghosting (slow pixel response); a bright or dark halo is overshoot from overdrive set too high.

Levels, banding and sharpness. The level squares are 1–20 steps above black and below white on the usual 0–255 scale. The ramps use 256 steps per colour, so an 8-bit screen shows at most very faint steps. The sharpness patterns are drawn at the size of your screen's physical pixels; with display scaling such as 125% or 150%, single-pixel lines turn grey or shimmer — normal, and not a fault.

Privacy. Everything runs in your browser. The screen details come from standard browser properties and are only shown to you, or copied when you ask.
