🎒 Evacuation & Go-Bag Planner

Three different emergencies need three different bags, and most people prepare the wrong one. Build all three here, weigh them against what you can actually carry, and print a checklist. The point is not a perfect kit — it is a kit that exists, is packed, and is by the door.

The rule nobody follows: a bag you cannot carry is not a bag. Aim for no more than about a quarter of your body weight, and less if you will be walking far or carrying a child. A 20 kg pack feels manageable in the kitchen and unbearable after three kilometres.

The three bags, and why the first one is the one that matters

The grab bag is for leaving in minutes: a fire, a gas leak, a flood warning, a knock at the door. It should be small enough that you grab it without thinking, and it should contain the things you cannot replace and cannot do without — medication, documents, keys, cash, a phone and a charger. Most evacuations in the UK are this kind, and most people have none.

The 72-hour bag is for being away from home for a few days with nothing: water, food that needs no cooking, warmth, a torch, a radio, first aid, hygiene. Seventy-two hours is the standard because it is roughly how long it takes emergency services to reach everyone in a widespread incident.

The stay-put kit is for the opposite situation: you cannot leave, or leaving is more dangerous than staying. A power cut in winter, a road closure, a severe weather warning. This is water and food for two weeks, warmth without electricity, and a way to charge a phone. It is by far the cheapest of the three to prepare because it stays at home and can be heavy.

Whatever you pack, put a copy of your documents in a sealed waterproof bag or a USB stick, keep some cash in small notes (card terminals fail in a power cut), and check the bag every six months. Medicines expire, batteries leak, children grow, and a bag nobody has opened is a bag of surprises.
