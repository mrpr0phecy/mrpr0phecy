    How the names are built, and why they sound spooky

    How does the Halloween name generator work?

    Each name is assembled from three parts: a title for the style, your own name reshaped to fit it, and a surname built from a landscape word. Every part has a fixed meaning, so the tool can show you what the name literally says — Ashmoor is "ash tree by the moor", Grimshade is "grim shade" — as well as how it sounds.

    What makes a name sound spooky?

    Three things do most of the work: long back vowels (moor, grave, hollow), consonant clusters at the end (wick, thorne, shroud), and words borrowed from landscape and weather rather than from horror films. Add a title and the name stops being a word and becomes a character. Spookiness 5 leans on the harder clusters; 1 keeps your name almost intact.

    Why do so many of the names sound Transylvanian or Welsh?

    Because English place-name endings carry that weight already: -moor, -wick, -holm, -grave and -hurst are all real settlement or landscape suffixes, and they feel older than the person wearing them. Vampire fiction then borrowed the same sounds, which is why "von" plus a landscape word reads as gothic without naming any real place.

    Can I use the name for a costume or a story?

    Yes — it is invented for you, and it is not a real person's name. Use it on a name badge, a party invitation, a character sheet or a table plan. If you are writing a story, keep the name and the backstory line; the two together give a character a place and a job in one sentence.

    Are these names trademarked characters?

    No. Nothing here reuses a film, book or game character: every title, surname and epithet is generic folklore vocabulary. Similar-sounding names in fiction are coincidence, not reference, so a costume labelled with one of these will not collide with a licensed character.
