Why generate placeholders locally, and which sizes people actually need

No dependency on someone else’s server. Placeholder services work by putting a URL such as /600x400 in your markup. That is convenient until the service throttles, changes its domain or goes away — and several popular ones have — at which point every mock-up, test fixture and demo that pointed at it breaks at once. A file you generated yourself sits in your repository and keeps working offline, in CI and in a client demo on hotel Wi-Fi. For quick prototyping the Copy data URI button gives you a self-contained image you can paste straight into src or a CSS url(), and the SVG option is tiny and stays crisp at any zoom.

Sizes worth knowing. 1200×630 is the Open Graph / social share card; 1080×1080 a square social post; 1080×1920 a story or reel; 1920×1080 a full-HD hero or slide; 1280×720 a video thumbnail; 800×600 and 640×480 are the classic 4:3 content images; 300×250 (medium rectangle), 728×90 (leaderboard) and 160×600 (skyscraper) are the standard display-ad slots; 400×400 or 512×512 an avatar; 16×16 / 32×32 favicons. Tick 2× pixel density to export a file with twice the pixels for the same layout size, which is what a retina-ready srcset needs.

Patterns and labels. The diagonal cross is the traditional wireframe convention for “an image goes here”; the grid and checkerboard make it obvious when an image is being stretched or cropped by the layout; the gradient shows whether text overlays remain legible. The label defaults to the dimensions because knowing which slot rendered is the whole point of a placeholder — when you are laying out with real copy, type any label you like. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded or fetched.
