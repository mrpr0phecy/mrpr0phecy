Keeping GIFs small, what the settings do, and how the encoder works

File size. A GIF stores every frame as a full picture with at most 256 colours, so size grows with width × height × frames. The three levers, in order of effect: fewer frames (a shorter clip or a lower frame rate — 10 per second reads as smooth for most footage, 5 for reactions), a smaller width (480 px is the sweet spot for chat apps; Slack and Discord downscale anyway), and fewer colours. Dithering makes gradients and skin tones look right but adds noise that compresses badly; turn it off for screen recordings and flat graphics, where it also keeps text crisp. The estimate under the button is rough; the real number appears when the GIF is done.

Trimming. Drag the start and end sliders or scrub the video and press Start = current time. The GIF is sampled at the frame rate you choose from that range, so a 4-second clip at 10 fps is 40 frames. Speed changes how long each frame is shown; Boomerang plays the clip forward and then backward, the Instagram effect that hides the loop point.

The encoder. Frames are drawn to a canvas at the chosen width, a shared palette is built with median-cut quantisation from samples across the clip, each pixel is mapped to its nearest colour (with Floyd–Steinberg error diffusion when dithering is on), and the indices are compressed with LZW into GIF89a blocks with a Netscape loop extension. It is the same format every browser, phone and chat app has read since 1989 — no plugins, no server, no watermark. Clips are capped at 300 frames to keep the file and your memory sensible.

Formats. The video must be something your browser can play: MP4 (H.264) and WebM work everywhere; MOV from iPhones works in Safari and usually in Chrome; HEVC and AV1 depend on the device. If the video appears but stays black, the browser could not decode it — re-export it as MP4 first.
