What the numbers mean, and what a browser can and cannot see

Where the data comes from. The browser's Gamepad API reports each stick axis from −1 to 1 (for the vertical axis, −1 is up) and each button from 0 to 1, many times a second. This page reads it on every screen refresh and draws it. Nothing is recorded or sent, and the tool never asks for any permission — browsers simply hide controllers until you press a button, so a web page cannot quietly fingerprint the devices plugged into your computer.

Stick drift test. With your hands off, the tool waits two seconds for the sticks to settle, then samples for two more and reports how far each stick sits from dead centre — the average resting offset and the largest reading it saw. As a rough guide: under 0.03 is centred; 0.03–0.08 is a slight offset most games hide; 0.08–0.15 is drift you may notice as slow creep in games with a small deadzone; above 0.15 is drift that usually shows. The suggested deadzone is the largest reading plus a small margin. For scale, Microsoft's XInput documentation defines default deadzones of 7849 and 8689 out of 32767 for the left and right sticks (about 0.24 and 0.27); many games use smaller ones.

Circularity test. Roll each stick slowly around its outer edge a few times. The tool keeps the furthest reading in each of 72 five-degree slices and reports the average distance from a perfect circle of radius 1. A stick that reports a squarer shape (diagonals above 1) or falls short somewhere scores higher. Zero is a perfect circle; compare left against right, or against the same model, rather than chasing zero — some controllers are deliberately shaped by their gates.

Deadzone slider. The yellow disc shows a radial deadzone on the plots and the readout shows what a game using it would receive, rescaled so the edge still reaches 1. It changes nothing on the controller.

What a browser cannot tell you. The true USB or Bluetooth polling rate and input latency are hidden behind the browser, which also only reports what the operating system passes on. Button names follow the browser's “standard” layout; if your controller shows as non-standard, the diagram may not match, but the raw button and axis lists are exact. Vibration needs a browser that implements the Gamepad haptics API (Chromium browsers such as Chrome and Edge do) and a controller with rumble motors; trigger rumble is only offered where the browser says the controller supports it.
