How it works, which style to pick, and where it will look right

What happens. The image is shrunk to a grid of cells — one per character — and each cell's brightness picks a character from a ramp that runs from empty (a space) to dense (an @ or a full block). Because text characters are about twice as tall as they are wide, the grid uses half as many rows as a square grid would; Cell shape is that ratio, and you can nudge it if your font is unusually narrow or wide. Width is the number of characters per line: 60–100 fits a chat message, 150–300 is for wallpapers and prints.

Styles. The classic 10-level ramp (.:-=+*#%@) is the recognisable "ASCII art" look and survives any font. The 70-level ramp gives smoother tone but shows more texture. Unicode blocks (░▒▓█) read as a proper greyscale image and paste well into Discord, Slack and most editors. Braille uses the 2×4 dot grid of each braille character, so one text cell holds eight pixels — it is by far the sharpest, and the dither setting matters most there. Two-tone is for stencil-like results and old terminals.

Dark or light. On a dark background, bright parts of the picture should be the densest characters; on white paper it is the other way round. Pick where you will paste it and the mapping flips accordingly; Invert flips it again if you prefer the negative. Dithering spreads the rounding error from each cell into its neighbours (Floyd–Steinberg), which turns flat bands into smooth gradients at the price of a little speckle. It is on by default for braille and two-tone, where it is essential, and off for the character ramps, where most people prefer the cleaner poster look — the choice is remembered per style.

Where it works. ASCII art only lines up in a monospace font. Terminals, code blocks, Notepad, VS Code and Discord or Slack messages wrapped in triple backticks are all fine; a normal email or word-processor font will scramble it unless you switch the text to Courier or Consolas. Very wide art will wrap in narrow windows — the PNG download avoids all of that, and Colour paints each character in the colour of its part of the image.
