🪜 Ground Ladder Placement & Reach

Quarter rule, climbing angle from where the butt actually sits, the height a given ladder reaches, the ladder you need for a given objective, and the collapse zone for the building.

Why 75 degrees, the straight-arm check, and the angles that are deliberately not 75

The quarter rule. Put the butt out one quarter of the working length and the ladder sits at acos(¼) = 75.5° from the ground. That angle is the standard because it gives good stability, puts the least bending stress on the beams, and lets a firefighter stand upright on the rungs at arm's length from them — the same posture as walking on flat ground. The field check is the straight-arm check: heels at the butt tips, arms out horizontally, hands should just touch the rung in front of you. Every ground ladder carries that diagram on the beam.

Reach. The working length is the hypotenuse, so the height actually reached is L × sin(θ) — about 97 % of the working length at the quarter rule, not the full length. A 24-foot extension ladder fully extended reaches roughly 23 feet of height with its butt 6 feet out. Working length also excludes the overlap between sections: on a two-section ladder each click of the pawl is about 14 inches, on a three-section about 28 inches, and the beams must always be engaged by the pawls.

Tip placement by objective. Roof access: the tip goes 3 to 5 rungs above the roofline, which gives a safe and comfortable transition onto the roof — and matches the OSHA requirement in 29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(1) that side rails extend at least 3 feet above a landing. Window access: the tip at or just below the windowsill, so the ladder is visible from inside to anyone needing it, and the tip just inside the sill for an interior egress ladder. Working at height on a leg lock: 75°.

Angles that are deliberately not 75. About 65° for an egress ladder placed in a window for bailout or climb-out — shallower is easier to get onto under pressure. Around 60° for VEIS and emergency egress, and for the head-first ladder slide taught in firefighter survival. Shallow angles also make removing an unconscious occupant, or a downed firefighter, considerably more controllable. These are all outside the textbook optimum and that is the point: pick the angle for the task, not the default.

Collapse zone. The conventional fireground rule is a collapse zone of 1.5 times the height of the building — a 40-foot wall throws debris out to 60 feet. Ladder placement, apparatus placement, personnel staging and the collapse-zone officer's boundary all work off that figure.

What this cannot see. Soft or uneven ground, ice, a butt that walks when the first firefighter puts weight on it, a ladder raised against a parapet rather than the roof deck, fire involvement directly below the placement, a ladder left unfooted on a slope, and a building that has already moved. The straight-arm check and a heeling firefighter beat any calculated angle. If the ladder has to be set at an angle this tool would flag as unsafe because there is no other option, the correct answer is a different placement, not a different calculation.
