How the cards are made, and how to run a game

Word cards. Every card is a fresh shuffle of your list, with no item twice on one card and no two cards alike. A 5 × 5 card with a free centre needs at least 24 items, 4 × 4 needs 16 and 3 × 3 needs 9 (8 with a free centre); more items make the cards more different from each other, so games last longer. Emoji work too — try 🐄 🚜 🌉 for pre-readers.

75-ball cards. The US format: column B holds five numbers from 1–15, I from 16–30, N four from 31–45 around the free centre, G from 46–60 and O from 61–75.

90-ball tickets. The UK format: each ticket has three rows of nine columns with fifteen numbers, five in every row; the first column holds 1–9, the next 10–19 and so on up to 80–90 in the last, sorted top to bottom, and every column has at least one number. Tickets come in strips of six that use every number from 1 to 90 exactly once — so with a full strip, every call marks something. Prizes usually go for one line, two lines and a full house.

Calling. Each game draws from a shuffled pool with no repeats. Keep the board on a big screen so players can catch up, and when someone shouts, type their card number into the checker: it rebuilds that card from the set code and marks what has been called, so you can confirm a line or full house in a second. The traditional calls are the classic British ones; a few that have not aged well are left out, and those numbers are called the plain way (“eight and eight, eighty-eight”). Reading aloud uses only voices built into your device, so nothing you call is sent anywhere; if your device has none, the option stays off.

Printing. The PDF is made in your browser, one image per page at 150 dots per inch, which is crisp on any home printer. Print at 100% (“actual size”). Your lists and games stay on this device.
