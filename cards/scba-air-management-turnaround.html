🫁 SCBA Air Management & Turnaround Pressure

What the "30 minute" label actually means, EOSTI, and why the rule of thirds is a floor not a plan

The rated duration is not a firefighting duration. A 30-minute cylinder holds about 45 cubic feet — roughly 1,274 litres — and the rating comes from a NIOSH breathing machine drawing 40 litres per minute at 24 breaths per minute in a controlled test. Real firefighting runs 60 to 79 litres per minute at the sharp end, and measured depletion of a 30-minute, 4,500 psi cylinder under working conditions is commonly 12 to 16 minutes. Treat the label as a capacity specification and nothing more.

Rule of thirds. One third of the air to get in, one third to get out, one third held as an unbreachable reserve for the emergency. On a 4,500 psi cylinder that is 1,500 psi each. The turnaround point is where you begin the exit — not where you start looking for the way out, and not where you decide you should have started.

EOSTI. The end-of-service-time indicator — the bell, the vibrating PASS, the heads-up display — is set by NFPA 1970 to activate at 34 % of rated pressure on a 2,216 psi cylinder, 31 % on a 4,500 psi cylinder and 29 % on a 5,500 psi cylinder. Older apparatus may be set to the legacy 25 % or 35 %. When it goes off you are out of air: the remaining pressure is reserve, not working air.

Turnaround pressure. Turnaround psi = ((entry − EOSTI psi) ÷ 2) + EOSTI psi. Worked example: a 4,500 psi cylinder with a 25 % EOSTI (1,125 psi). (4,500 − 1,125) ÷ 2 + 1,125 = 1,687.5 + 1,125 = 2,812 psi, conventionally rounded down to 2,800. Below that number, you are leaving.

Consumption rate. Air consumption rate in psi per minute = psi used ÷ minutes worked. Cylinder duration = rated fill pressure ÷ ACR. Example: 2,000 psi used in 15 minutes gives an ACR of 133 psi/min, and 4,500 ÷ 133 = 34 minutes of total cylinder life — of which a third is already gone the moment you commit to using it. Time each firefighter on every drill, keep the number, and use that individual's figure rather than a department average.

What this cannot see. Adrenaline and workload raise consumption by a factor of two or more without warning. Search, forcible entry, going up stairs with a charged line, a mayday and an entanglement all change the arithmetic mid-task. The rule of thirds is a floor to work down to, not a plan to aim at — a department that plans its work to exactly one third has no reserve left for the thing that went wrong. Nothing here accounts for a buddy whose air you may have to share, and no figure on this page substitutes for the gauge on your chest, your PAR accountability, or your department's air management SOP.
