    How the simulation works, who it affects, and how to design around it

    Who. Red–green colour vision deficiency affects roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women of Northern European descent (it is X-linked, hence the imbalance). The commonest form by far is deuteranomaly — weakened green cones — at around 5% of men; protanomaly, protanopia and deuteranopia are each about 1%. Tritan (blue–yellow) defects are rare, roughly 1 in 10,000, and affect both sexes equally. Achromatopsia, seeing no colour at all, is rarer still at about 1 in 30,000; it is included here because a grey rendering is also the fastest test of whether your design depends on hue alone.

    How. Each pixel is converted from sRGB to linear light, multiplied by a 3×3 matrix from Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes' physiologically based model (IEEE TVCG, 2009), and converted back. Their model is built from cone spectral sensitivities and the opponent-colour stage of human vision, and it produces one matrix per type and severity; the slider picks from their published table in steps of 10%, where 100% is dichromacy (a cone type missing entirely) and lower values are anomalous trichromacy (a cone type shifted). Achromatopsia is the relative luminance of each pixel. The result is what a person with normal vision would see that is equivalent to what the colour-blind viewer sees — it cannot show you their experience, only which colours collapse together.

    The palette check. Every colour you enter is pushed through the same matrices, then converted to CIE L*a*b*, and each pair's difference (ΔE, CIE76) is measured. A ΔE below about 10 between two data colours means people will struggle to tell adjacent bars, lines or map regions apart; below 5 they will not manage at all. The default palette is a common charting default and fails for red–green viewers; Try a safe palette loads Okabe and Ito's eight colours, designed to stay distinct under all three dichromacies.

    Designing around it. Never let colour be the only carrier of meaning: add labels, patterns, shapes or direct annotation. Vary lightness as well as hue — pairs that differ in lightness survive every simulation, which is why the grey view is so revealing. Avoid red-versus-green for good/bad states; blue-versus-orange does the same job for everyone. Check charts at the severity most people actually have (around 50–70%) as well as at 100%.
