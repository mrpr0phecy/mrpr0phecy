For Dew

Thirty-seven poems for Dew, written in the dark before morning and sent the long way round. Some are about water. Most are about you. There are ayaat in here, a locked page, and a sky that keeps moving.

before we start

Dew —

I could have bought flowers. Flowers are sensible: an afternoon, a card, done. They do not survive an ocean.

Instead there is this: a night sky that keeps its own weather, thirty-seven poems, the verses I kept going back to, and a last page that opens with your name — or, where you are, with the word for dew.

You like black and white, so the page mostly is: ink, paper, silver, and the only colour in it is the sky's. You are studious, so there are two clocks at the bottom of this letter, keeping your time and mine. There is music, if you want it — a slow thing in D minor, written into the page. The button is at the top right and it is entirely your choice.

Take your time. There is no timer. It is a website; it will wait, which is more than I can say for either of us.

— M.

xi

Sonnet at Six

sonnet · 14 lines

The lawn is holding light it cannot keep;the hedge stands silver, not yet green or grey;a blackbird wakes the street from half-sleepand finds the morning not quite on its way.I have been up since four, and cannot saywhat kept me — only that the sky went deepand something in the quiet made me stayto watch the ordinary learn to leap.

You'll ask me, later, what I did all night.I'll say: I made a small machine of glassthat holds a drop of light and holds it right,and taught it how to turn as hours pass.

You'll laugh and call it strange. It's true. And yetthe drop is just the morning's alphabet.

Two Seas

a barrier, and no partition

Allah set two seas beside each otherand put between them a barrierneither one crosses.

One is sweet. One is salt.They do not mix. They do not stoptouching, either, along the whole length of the world.

I think about that more than I should:two people, one ocean,a line the size of everythingand no crossing —

and still the same rainfalling into both of us.

after Surah Al-Furqan, 25:53

xiv

Seven Hours (Six in Summer)

the planet’s one joke

The planet keeps six hours between usin the summer, and seven in the dark halfof the year, which is the closest thingto a joke gravity has ever made.

When you wake I am finishing the evening.When I sleep you are in the middle of your day.Between us: one long Tuesdaythat neither of us has to do alone.

The Ocean Between

rindu

The word for missing someone, in your language,is not a sad word. It is small: rindu.It works like the tide —it comes in without asking,it goes out without apologising,and it has been doing thissince before either of us had a name for it.

xviii

Field Guide to Her

notes toward a species

Habitat. Anywhere with a window, a book, a kettle, or the rain arriving.

Voice. Low in the morning, quick by ten, dangerous after the second coffee.

Diet. Tea. Other people’s leftovers. The last ten minutes of any film.

Reading. Three books at once, one of them in another language, all of them annotated in the margin like she is arguing with the author and winning.

Behaviour. Will not walk past a cat without speaking to it. Laughs before the joke lands. Claims not to be brave, then says the true thing in a room full of people who were avoiding it. Remembers everyone’s drink and nobody’s birthday. Prays, and then makes tea, in that order.

Conservation. Least concerned, which is the problem. Someone should be concerned. I am concerned. Consider this the concern.

Notes for observers. Do not mistake quiet for agreement. The key is under the mat, and she has known all along.

compiled from evidence, not hope

Black and White

the two things that are true

You like black and white, so:here is the whole worldwith the colour turned downto the two things that are true —the dark, and the light it is not.

Everything else is weather.Everything else is the argumentwe have with the sky about what shade Tuesday is.

I like you in the low light.I like you in the high contrast.I like you in the part of the photographthe dark did not reach.

The Studious Hour

a hum, not a silence

Glasses pushed up. Pen between two fingerslike a cigarette she has never smoked.Two books open, one of them wrong,the other earning its keep.

This is the woman the room rearranges itself aroundwithout being asked.This is the part of the eveningI would move house for.

There is a particular quietwhen someone is thinking well.It is not silence, it is a hum,and I have never heard it in anyone else.

xxi

Forty-Two

said quietly, in writing

Forty-two is not a number you say out loud.So I am writing it down.

Forty-two years of becoming herself:the opinions she changed,the books she finished out of spite,the countries she has been kind to,the mornings she got up anyway.

That is the whole poem.The rest is just me, putting it down carefully.

not a compliment. an inventory

Embun

In your language, dew is embun —two soft syllables and a hush,and it means the same thing:the little water that comes from nowhereand makes the morning expensive.

I learned the word for youbefore I learned anything else that day,which is how I knowthe language was rightand I was going to be late for everything else.

Seven Sealed Secrets

turn them over · the order is not random · nor are the first letters

Seven small admissions about the man who made this page. He was asked to explain, tastefully, why he is worth the trouble. He has delegated the job to a series of sealed cards, which is either modesty or cowardice. Open them in order. The first letters of the seven are hiding a word — and it is a word you will be using shortly anyway.

Field Notes on the Author

seven observations · kept by nobody in particular

He refused to write this section himself, on the grounds that a man listing his own virtues is a man you should edge away from at a party. So these are observations, in the third person, which is the only way he could be persuaded. Read the first letters downwards: seven notes, one word, and it is his one good quality — and, as it happens, a word the Qur’an returns to more than ninety times. Sabr. Patience. He would like it noted that he is not naturally good at it; he is only good at it for her.

Note i

Patient in the way that looks like stubbornness: he will give an entire evening to a two-degree rotation that nobody will ever notice, and call it a good evening.

Note ii

Accurate to the point of being annoying. He will tell you the thing is forty-seven pixels out of place. It is forty-seven pixels out of place.

Note iii

Terrible at leaving a thing half-finished. The locked page at the end of this is his idea of a joke, and also his idea of a hand held out.

Note iv

Interested. Ask him about surface tension at one in the morning and cancel the rest of your evening; bring a chair. Better still, tell him what you are reading — he will have found a copy before you finish the sentence, in English if he can, and he will have opinions, and he will want yours more.

Note v

Embarrassingly easy to please. One laugh from you and the whole week is accounted for. He will deny that this is a weakness.

Note vi

Not, in fact, arrogant — but he did ask for a section explaining why he is worth the trouble, which is its own kind of evidence, and he knows it, and that is why it is written down here instead of by him.

Note vii

Trying, quietly, all the time, at things that don't look like effort from the outside. That is the trick of him: it always looks easy afterwards. It is never easy at the time.

The Locked Page

one word · one syllable · you have had it all along

One word opens it.

In English it is one syllable. It is on the cover of this page, in the first letters of the poem that closes part four, in the first letters of the seven secrets, and in the seventh riddle. In your language it is two soft syllables and a hush, and I learned that word before I learned anything else that year. It is also your name, which makes this the easiest lock ever built. The point was never the lock.

four hints, if the night is being difficult
  1. It falls before morning, and it is not rain.
  2. It is the name of the person this page was built for — and the word for it, where she is.
  3. Look at the first letters of the acrostic in part four. Then look at the top of the page.
  4. Or say it in her language: embun. That works too. It was always going to work.
