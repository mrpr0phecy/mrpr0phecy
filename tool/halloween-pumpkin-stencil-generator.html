      How the pattern, the bridges and the timings are worked out

      What size should a pumpkin stencil be?

      A flat print meets a round pumpkin, so a stencil the full width of the fruit wraps too far round and distorts at the edges. This tool scales the pattern to 78% of the pumpkin's widest point — for a 24 cm pumpkin that is an 18.7 cm stencil — which covers the flat face without bending round the sides.

      Why do the bridges matter?

      Every island of pumpkin flesh between two cut-outs is a bridge, and a bridge narrower than about 6 mm snaps when the pumpkin dries. The tool sets the minimum at 6 mm, or 1 mm for every 3.6 cm of pumpkin width once the fruit is wider than 21.6 cm: 6.7 mm on a 24 cm pumpkin, 11.1 mm on a 40 cm one. Fine detail earns its look by keeping those gaps.

      How is the carve time estimated?

      Five minutes to tape and trace, two minutes for clean-up, and then a per-cut figure that rises with difficulty: about 3.5 minutes a shape on easy, 4.5 on medium and 6 on intricate, because fine shapes are cut more slowly and more carefully. It is a planning figure for a first-time carver with a serrated pumpkin saw.

      Which knife should I use?

      A dedicated serrated pumpkin saw, not a kitchen knife: the blade is short, the teeth clear pulp and the handle keeps your hand out of the opening. Cut in short strokes with the blade pointing away from you, and never put a hand inside the pumpkin while the saw is moving.

      Is the stencil reusable?

      Yes. Print it, tape it to the flattest face, and prick through the outline every centimetre with a pin or skewer. Lift the paper off and join the dots with a marker — or dust the holes with flour — and you can carve the same pattern again next year without a second print.
