2026-09-14 — Ten tools for ten subjects the catalogue had nothing on

An audit of all 1149 tools found whole subjects with no tool at all. Ten new ones fill them: neurodivergent time, food allergens, yarn, tinnitus, childcare costs, parking appeals, rail delays, Gift Aid, negotiation and LaTeX. 1159 tools.

  • ADHD Time & Task Lab — log estimate against actual, learn your own time multiplier, translate “ten minutes” into what it really takes, and open a stuck task with a five-rung ladder.
  • Ingredient Label Allergen Decoder — paste an ingredient list and it names the 14 declarable allergens hiding in it: caseinate is milk, arachis oil is peanut, E220–E228 are sulphites. Runs offline; the label never leaves the page.
  • Knitting & Crochet Yarn Studio — cast-on at your gauge, yarn needed from a weighed swatch, leftover-ball maths, and wraps-per-inch to hook size.
  • Tinnitus & Hearing Relief Studio — soundscapes generated live in the browser, a pitch match and notch filter, a session timer that fades out, exposure limits and the red flags that need same-day care.
  • UK Childcare Cost Calculator — funded hours, Tax-Free Childcare and Universal Credit childcare, with the £100k cliff, the 38-week gap and whether another hour of work actually pays. Every rate is an editable box.
  • UK Parking Appeal Builder — a council PCN and a private parking charge look identical and obey different rules. Dates the deadlines from the notice, weighs fighting it against paying the discount, and drafts the appeal.
  • Rail Delay Repay Calculator — the 15/30/60/120-minute bands for single, return and season tickets, the 28-day claim window, and what a year of commuting delays is worth.
  • Gift Aid Calculator — the 25p uplift, what a higher-rate donor claims back, the tax-you-must-have-paid rule with a shortfall warning, and the small-donations scheme with its three caps.
  • Negotiation Prep Studio — target, walk-away, BATNA and their ceiling become a one-page brief: the anchor, the zone of agreement, three concessions to trade, and scripts for opening and for a lowball.
  • LaTeX Table & Maths Studio — paste a table, get booktabs LaTeX with the alignment chosen from the data and every special character escaped properly, plus an escaper and a snippet library.

All five are self-contained fragments: no network calls, no sign-up, no tracking. Time lab · Allergen decoder · Yarn studio · Tinnitus studio · Childcare costs · Parking appeals · Delay Repay · Gift Aid · Negotiation brief · LaTeX tables

  • 24 Sept 2026

    Six new tools — tier lists, fancy text, controllers, screens, bingo and tallies Feature Fix

    Six things people look for every day that the catalogue did not have. Each one runs entirely in your browser, and each was tested with a real browser clicking, dragging, typing and exporting — not just loaded.

    • Tier List Maker — rank anything from S to F by dragging, tapping or with the keyboard; add your own photos; download a 2,400-pixel PNG, or send a link that carries the list itself. The person you send it to chooses whether to load it, so a link never overwrites their own board.
    • Fancy Text Generator — 51 Unicode styles for bios and names. Every letter was checked against the official character tables, including the gaps in the script, Fraktur and double-struck alphabets where careless generators show empty boxes — and it says plainly that screen readers may spell these letters out one by one.
    • Gamepad Tester — every button, trigger and stick live on a controller diagram that uses Xbox, PlayStation or Nintendo names, a timed stick-drift test with a suggested deadzone, a circularity score, and rumble tests.
    • Monitor Test — measures your real refresh rate, then full-screen colours for dead and stuck pixels, backlight bleed, uniformity and touch; moving patterns for motion blur; level steps that spot the full-versus-limited RGB mix-up; banding and sharpness patterns. Nothing flashes on its own.
    • Bingo Card Generator — word, 75-ball and UK 90-ball cards as a PDF, with a caller. 90-ball tickets come in proper strips of six that use every number exactly once, and every set has a code, so the caller can type a winning card's number and see it marked against the calls.
    • Tally Counter — a big-button clicker with several named counters, targets, a timed log with the count rate, CSV export and a full-screen mode for counting people through a door.

    A fix that reaches 87 existing tools. The tool page fades each tool in with a short animation, and that animation was left holding its final position. Browsers treat an element holding a transform — even one that moves nothing — as the frame for everything inside it that asks to be fixed to the screen. So in 87 tools, pop-up notices, dialogs and full-screen views were pinned to the tool's box instead of the window: one tool's "saved" notice, meant for the top corner of the screen, sat 380 pixels above it once you had scrolled. The animation now lets go when it ends, and a test fails if it ever holds on again.

    Three checks that guard every tool were fixed on the way. The start-up check rejected the one-line start-up pattern the contributor notes recommend; the code reader behind two checks mistook a pattern written after return for a division, which could hide everything below it from those checks; and add-tool.sh now stages a new tool before building, so the sitemap lists it on the first run instead of failing.

  • 24 Sept 2026

    A logo that belongs to the site it sits on Feature

    The mark from 21 September was legible, but it did not look like it came from here. The home page is a dark instrument panel — hairlines, small tracked capitals, a tick scale, a search console with bracketed corners — and the one bright, glossy, glowing object on it was the logo. A magnifier holding a sparkle is also the icon every "AI search" product uses.

    The new mark is drawn out of the page: the two cyan corner brackets from the search console, holding a white four-point star on a dark tile. The brackets find; the star is what they find. It is the same drawing in the browser tab, on a phone's home screen, in the social card and on the home page, and it still reads at 16 pixels because the cyan and the white stand far apart from the dark tile.

    The site's name is now a proper lockup — the mark, a hairline, and the name on two lines with useful in cyan — in the hero, in the footer and in the downloadable logo files. The top bar of every other page (the guides, the blog, the category and tool pages, and more — about seventy in all) used an emoji where the logo should be; they now show the mark.

    Two fixes came with it. The brand sheet claimed a cyan for text on white backgrounds that was nowhere near readable enough (2.9:1); it is now a darker cyan that passes (4.6:1), and the logo generator refuses to run if it ever slips. And one blog post's "back to the tools" links went to the blog's own index instead of the tools.

  • 17 Sept 2026

    The home page stops re-downloading its own stylesheet on every visit Fix

    The entire look of the home page lived inside the page itself: about 118 KB of styling that every visit downloaded again, and that the browser had to receive in full before it could paint anything at all. It is now two ordinary cached files. The part the first screen needs (~82 KB) still arrives before anything is painted, and the part nothing can see yet — the palette and contributions panels, the toolbox, the grid/list modes, the directory view, the maximise window, the "no results" box and the footer — is fetched alongside it but applied only after the first paint.

    What you should notice: the page paints noticeably sooner on a slow connection, repeat visits re-send about 14 KB instead of 33 KB, and the very first frame is no longer gated on a render-blocking hint that waited for the tool grid's empty container to be parsed — 30 KB of markup (16% of the page) after the styles.

    Both files, and the page's own script, are now requested with a version tag that is kept in step with the service worker, so a page can never end up running against a previous deploy's styles or code. A new check fails the build if a first-paint rule is ever moved into the deferred file, if the rules that keep those panels hidden move out of it, or if the whole sheet is inlined again.

    The page's application code was split the same way. Everything you only meet after a click — the palette and contributions panels, the shared toolbox, the maximise window and the alternate directory view — used to be compiled before the first tool appeared. It now loads quietly in the background once the page is up (or the moment you click something that needs it), which gives a slow phone more room for the cards themselves. Reader mode stays immediate, since it restyles every card.

    Offline visits got the same treatment: the page's styles and script are now saved as part of the offline copy, so a page opened without a connection still looks and works like the page — before, the saved page came up without its styling or its card loader.

    The rest of the page was audited for the same reason. Long explanations that used to sit in HTML comments — invisible to you, but compressed into every single download — moved into the project's architecture notes, and a render-blocking hint that made the browser wait for the tool grid's empty container before drawing anything was removed. The document itself is now about 64 KB instead of 185 KB.

  • 17 Sept 2026

    New tools appear on the first visit; scrolling stops measuring 1,190 cards a frame Fix Tooling

    The service worker used to hand back the copy of the tool list it already had, and refresh it in the background for next time. So every batch of new tools went live on the site while a returning visitor's grid still showed the list from their previous visit — and only a second visit fixed it. The tool list, the card files and the home page's own script are now only served from cache while they are inside the ten-minute window GitHub Pages itself allows; after that the live copy wins, and the cache is only the fallback if the network is slow or gone.

    Five more tools were injecting mobile rules meant for this page's grid (.dashboard .card) from their own script — rules the pre-ship check could not see, because it only read <style> blocks. Those rules are gone, and the check now reads styles injected from script too.

    Speed. While you scroll with all the loader's slots busy, the fallback sweep used to measure every pending card — 1,190 forced layout reads on every single frame (measured: 71,404 over 60 frames), all to find cards it could not start, because the slots were occupied. It now only measures when a load slot is free. Six cards load at once instead of four. The speculation rules no longer prerender a whole standalone tool page when the mouse crosses a card, and no longer queue six tool-page prefetches on every keystroke in the search box. And the service worker no longer downloads the app script, the risk-notice script and both fonts a second time on install — 133 KB of fonts plus the scripts, bypassing the HTTP cache, while the first tools were still arriving.

  • 17 Sept 2026

    Cards stopped going missing; the home page loads lighter Fix Tooling

    Twenty tools shipped CSS written as if they had the page to themselves — a bare .card { background: white; max-width: 500px }, a body { padding: 40px }. On the home page every tool shares one document, so the moment one of those scrolled into view it restyled every other card on the grid: white boxes, squeezed widths, headers pushed out of sight. It looked like cards had vanished, and nothing was logged anywhere. Each of those tools now keeps its styles under its own root, and a new pre-ship check (scripts/check-card-css-leaks.py) fails the build if a tool ever does it again.

    Returning visitors could also be stuck on an old copy of the home page's script: the service worker cached it forever, so new tools were listed but the code driving the grid never updated. Scripts and catalogue files now refresh in the background on every visit. The home page also stops downloading things it never used — the 548 KB search index no longer competes with the first tools you see (it loads when you reach for the search box, or when the browser is idle), a duplicate copy of it and the 390 KB tools index are no longer prefetched, and every card no longer holds its own GPU layer for the life of the page, which is what made the grid heavier the further you scrolled.

    Production monitor stops crying false 404 on %-filenames Fix

    The post-deploy monitor reported a published coordination file as missing: a filename containing a literal % must be requested doubly-encoded, because the serving stack decodes %2F to a slash before routing.       • 9 Sept 2026 · 77a3c5f

        Riley: Goblin Tide — she was never on screen, and now the game plays like Zelda Fix Feature Tooling

        The report was blunt: "terrible to play, and I cannot see sprites". Both were true, and the second one had a single cause.

        • Nobody was drawn — Riley and every goblin are built from instanced parts sorted into buckets, and the renderer only ever flushed the buckets tagged as glowing: eyes, glasses, the wand tip. Robes, limbs, heads, hats, goblin bodies, loot gems and shots were batched every frame and thrown away, so the arena was a floating pair of glasses being chased by pairs of red eyes. One flush now draws every non-empty bucket, additive halos and telegraphs are drawn after the solid bodies instead of before, and a leaked instanced-attribute divisor that was corrupting the terrain's vertex colours from frame two onwards is reset per program. A regression test now fails if any body bucket is still holding instances after a frame.
        • Zelda controls (new default) — the camera follows you on its own: it swings in behind a committed run, keeps its framing through a fight and never spins chasing a sidestep. TAB Z-targets the goblin ahead (again to release), X cycles, and when the target dies the lock hands itself to the next goblin within 15u so a crowd fight flows. A click attacks — at your target if you have one, otherwise at whichever goblin is in the cone you are facing, otherwise straight ahead — so there is no crosshair to manage. The mouse orbits the camera if the browser hands over the pointer, and the game is fully playable if it does not (Esc frees it and is remembered).
        • Cursor aim kept — L or the pause toggle switches back to the previous scheme (camera follows the cursor, shots fly at it). Saved with the other camera settings.
        • The bot was steering half-wrong — the test hook that reports the camera basis read the view matrix with the wrong stride and returned a mirrored forward vector, so the playtest bot had been running to the world's edge since it was written and every earlier "bot reached wave N" number was measured on a bot fighting its own controls. Fixed, along with its mouse sign; it now plays either scheme (playtest.js 180 report zelda|mouse) and both reach wave 10 in 180 seconds on the same seed where it used to reach wave 6.

        Riley: Goblin Tide — the camera gets rebuilt, the combat gets a spine Feature Fix

        The 3D arena battler had a camera that fought the player: it snapped, sank into hills, ran backwards while you ran forwards and zoomed itself into Riley's skull. It is now a spring-damped orbit rig, and the whole game got a pass for feel.

        • Camera — smoothed orbit pivot, boom that lifts over props and terrain instead of cutting to a first-person close-up, FOV that widens when buried, over-the-shoulder framing only while aiming or locked on, and an auto-frame that swings behind Riley when you commit to a run.         • Controls fix — strafe was mirrored against the view projection, so A and D were swapped; the input basis is now derived from the same matrix the renderer uses, with a headless test that pins each key to a screen edge.
        • Combat — a three-hit melee chain (jab, jab, 360° cleave that launches the crowd), a dedicated melee input, perfect-dodge reward (dash through a wind-up and time crawls while magic floods back), a landing shockwave when you drop from height, and a last-stand surge on your final heart.
        • Fairness — an attack's wind-up can no longer hurt you: the red flare means "move now", and every committed attack leaves a recovery window to punish. Spitters telegraph before they fire. Corpses tumble instead of blinking out.
        • Engine — the sim is now a strictly fixed 60 Hz tick with interpolated rendering, so frame pacing, hit-stop and slow-motion no longer change how far Riley travels or where the goblins land (the determinism hash proves it).
